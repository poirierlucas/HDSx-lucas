# Plain-Language Summary

**For:** members of the community health board deciding whether to ask staff for a follow-up analysis of weight among lower-income adults.

We looked at body mass index (BMI), a common measure of weight relative to height, for adults age 20 to 80 in a classroom copy of a national U.S. health survey (NHANES). We focused on adults in lower-income households, meaning family income below 1.3 times the poverty line.

In this dataset, the average BMI of lower-income adults went up by about 1.6 points, from 28.8 in 1999-2000 to 30.4 in 2021-2023. The rise was fairly steady after the mid-2000s. Middle- and higher-income adults also went up, by about 1.3 points each, so the increase is not limited to lower-income adults.

These results have limits. They are simple averages of the people in the file and do not use the survey weights, so they do not describe all U.S. adults. They show a change over time, not its cause, and they do not show that income causes higher BMI. People with no recorded BMI or income were left out, and the file has no 2019-2020 survey.

Our suggested next step is for the board to ask staff for an analysis that uses the survey weights and design, with uncertainty ranges, for all three income groups. That would show whether the rise holds for U.S. adults and whether lower-income adults are moving away from the other groups.
